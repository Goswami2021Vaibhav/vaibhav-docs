Company & Role Specific Questions

1. How do you actually research a company before an interview, practically?​

📖 Introduction​

Nearly every question in this chapter depends on having done real research beforehand — this comes first because it's the preparation the rest of the chapter assumes you've already done.

🧭 Where to Actually Look​

The company's own website, especially an "about" or mission page, and its recent blog posts or engineering posts if it has them, for how it describes itself in its own words. Recent news coverage or press releases, for anything current — a funding round, a launch, an expansion — that might reasonably come up. The specific product or service itself, used firsthand where possible, since nothing communicates genuine interest better than direct, personal familiarity with what they actually make.

🎯 Look for One or Two Specific, Genuine Hooks, Not Exhaustive Trivia​

The goal isn't memorizing every fact available — it's finding one or two things that genuinely interest you and that you can speak to naturally, which is what actually comes across as authentic interest rather than a recited research summary.

💎 Good to Know: 30–60 Minutes of Focused Research Covers Nearly Everything This Chapter's Questions Actually Need​

This doesn't require days of preparation — a focused hour looking at the company's own materials, recent news, and the product itself is generally enough to answer every question in this chapter with genuine specificity.

2. "Why do you want to work at this company specifically?"​

📖 Introduction​

This is the single most direct test of whether you actually did the research from the previous question, or are giving an answer that could apply to any company in the same industry.

🎯 Name Something Genuinely Specific to This Company​

A specific product decision, mission, technical approach, or aspect of how the company operates — something that wouldn't be equally true of its closest competitor — is what actually distinguishes a researched answer from a generic one.

📝 Example Answer​

"I've been genuinely impressed by [specific product/approach/decision] — it's a different approach than most others in the space take, and it lines up with [specific reason it resonates with you]. That, combined with [a second specific, genuine reason], is what makes this opportunity stand out to me specifically."

💎 Good to Know: "Great Company Culture" and "Exciting Growth" Are the Two Most Overused, Least Convincing Answers to This Question​

Both phrases are technically true of a huge number of companies and communicate almost no genuine research or specific interest — if your answer to this question could be copy-pasted into an interview at a different company with only the name changed, it needs another pass.

3. "What do you know about our company or products?"​

📖 Introduction​

Unlike the "why do you want to work here" question, this one is checking factual accuracy and depth of research more directly, not just genuine interest.

🎯 Give an Accurate, Reasonably Specific Summary, Then a Point of Genuine Interest​

A brief, accurate description of what the company actually does, followed by one specific detail you found genuinely interesting during your research, demonstrates real preparation without turning into a recited encyclopedia entry.

🚫 What to Avoid​

Vague or slightly inaccurate descriptions are worse than admitting a gap — confidently misstating what a company does is a much stronger signal of not having done the research than a well-placed "I'd love to learn more about X specifically."

💎 Good to Know: This Question Is a Direct Payoff of the Research Covered Earlier in This Chapter​

Of every question in this chapter, this one most directly rewards or punishes whatever research you actually did beforehand — there's very little room to improvise a convincing answer here without real preparation.

4. "What do you think are the biggest challenges facing our company or industry right now?"​

📖 Introduction​

This is a more advanced research question, checking whether your understanding goes beyond the company's own marketing material into a genuine, informed perspective.

🎯 Base This on Real, Current Information, Not Speculation​

Something drawn from recent, credible news or industry coverage — a competitive pressure, a shift in the market, a widely discussed challenge in that space — is a far stronger basis for this answer than a guess dressed up as analysis.

🎯 Frame It Thoughtfully, Not as a Criticism​

Framing a genuine challenge as an interesting, current problem worth being aware of — rather than a pointed criticism of the company's choices — keeps the answer constructive and appropriate for an interview setting.

💎 Good to Know: You Don't Need a Perfect, Insider-Level Answer Here​

You're not expected to have a strategic-level view a company insider would have — a thoughtful, well-informed outsider's perspective, clearly grounded in real research, is exactly what this question is realistically looking for.

5. "How do you see yourself contributing in your first 90 days?"​

📖 Introduction​

This question checks whether you've thought concretely about ramping up in this specific role, rather than assuming you'll simply figure it out once you start.

🎯 Describe a Reasonable Learning Period Before Claiming Immediate Impact​

A credible early period focused on understanding the team, the codebase or systems, and how things currently work, before claiming to drive major changes, reads as more realistic and more experienced than promising sweeping impact from day one.

📝 Example Answer​

"In the first few weeks, I'd focus on understanding how the team currently works and getting familiar with the codebase and existing systems, likely through some smaller, well-scoped tasks. From there, I'd expect to start taking on more substantial ownership, ideally contributing to [a specific area relevant to the role] by the end of that first quarter."

💎 Good to Know: Promising Immediate, Sweeping Change Is a Common Overreach in This Answer​

A candidate who claims to be ready to overhaul processes or drive major change in their first week or two, without yet understanding the existing context, tends to read as less experienced, not more — a credible ramp-up period is a stronger signal of judgment.

6. "What do you know about our competitors, and how do we compare?"​

📖 Introduction​

This is a deeper research question than most, and it's fine to answer it at whatever depth your genuine research actually supports.

🎯 Name One or Two Real Competitors and a Genuine Point of Distinction​

Knowing the general competitive landscape, and being able to name a specific, genuine difference in approach or positioning — rather than a vague "you're all pretty similar" — shows real market awareness relevant to the business you'd be joining.

🚫 If You Genuinely Don't Know the Competitive Landscape Well​

Being honest that this is an area you haven't researched as deeply, while pivoting to what you do know or are eager to learn, is a better response than guessing at a competitor comparison you're not actually confident in.

💎 Good to Know: This Question Is Asked Far More Often for Product, Business, and Leadership-Adjacent Roles​

Purely technical roles are asked this less often than product, sales, or leadership-track roles — worth calibrating how much time you invest researching this specific angle based on the kind of role you're actually interviewing for.

7. "What excites you most about this specific role's responsibilities?"​

📖 Introduction​

This is a more granular version of "why do you want this job," focused specifically on the day-to-day content of the role rather than the company as a whole.

🎯 Reference a Specific Line From the Job Description or Role Discussion​

Pointing to a specific responsibility mentioned in the job posting or earlier in the interview process, and explaining genuinely why it appeals to you, shows you've actually read and thought about the role itself, not just applied broadly.

📝 Example Answer​

"The part of the role focused on [specific responsibility from the job description] stood out to me immediately — it's very close to the kind of work I've been trying to do more of, and [brief, genuine reason it appeals to you specifically]."

💎 Good to Know: This Question Pairs Naturally With the Projects Chapter's Advice on Closing a Story With a Role Connection​

The same instinct the Projects & Technical Discussions chapter recommends for closing a project story — an explicit link back to the role — is essentially what this question is asking for directly and on its own.

8. What if you're asked something about the company you genuinely don't know or couldn't find during your research?​

📖 Introduction​

Even solid research won't cover everything an interviewer might ask — how you handle a genuine gap matters more than never having one.

🎯 Admit It Plainly, and Show Genuine Interest in Finding Out​

"That's not something I came across in my research — could you tell me more?" is a completely fine answer, and turning it into a genuine follow-up question shows real curiosity rather than embarrassment about the gap.

🚫 What to Avoid​

Bluffing a guess as though it were a known fact is a much worse outcome than an honest gap — if the guess turns out to be wrong, it reads as either dishonest or careless, whereas an honest "I don't know" costs very little.

💎 Good to Know: Turning a Gap Into a Genuine Question Is a Small Moment That Reads Well​

A gap in your knowledge, handled by asking a real, curious follow-up question back to the interviewer, can actually leave a positive impression — it demonstrates comfort with not knowing everything and genuine engagement with learning more.

9. "Do you have any questions for us?" How should you handle this closing question?​

📖 Introduction​

This nearly always comes at the end of an interview, and answering "no, I think you covered everything" is one of the most common, avoidable missed opportunities in the whole process.

🎯 Always Have at Least Two or Three Genuine Questions Ready​

Prepared questions about the team's current priorities, what success looks like in the role after six months, or what the interviewer personally enjoys about working there, all demonstrate real engagement — and having a few ready in advance avoids being caught without one in the moment.

🎯 Ask Something Genuinely Specific to This Conversation, Where Possible​

A question that references something specific the interviewer mentioned earlier in the conversation shows you were actually listening, and tends to land better than a generic, prepared question that could be asked in any interview.

💎 Good to Know: This Is Also a Genuine Opportunity to Evaluate Them, Not Just a Test You're Being Given​

Beyond how it reflects on you, this moment is also a real chance to learn something that actually helps you decide whether the role is right for you — treating it purely as a test to pass, rather than a genuine two-way exchange, wastes a useful part of the conversation.

10. "What's your understanding of our company culture, and does it fit how you like to work?"​

📖 Introduction​

This checks two separate things at once — whether you've picked up on genuine signals about how the company actually operates, and whether you've honestly reflected on whether that fits you.

🎯 Base This on Genuine Signals, Not Guesses​

Details from the company's own materials, employee reviews, or things mentioned earlier in the interview process — about pace, collaboration style, or how decisions get made — are a much more credible basis for this answer than a generic guess about what a "good culture" probably looks like.

🎯 Connect It Honestly to How You Actually Work​

A genuine, specific reason a particular aspect of the culture fits how you like to work — rather than a blanket "I fit into any culture" — is both more credible and more useful information for the interviewer to actually evaluate fit against.

💎 Good to Know: A Genuine Mismatch Is Worth Raising as a Question, Not Hiding​

If something about the described culture genuinely gives you pause, this is a reasonable moment to ask a clarifying question about it rather than pretending it's a perfect fit — a genuine mismatch discovered after joining is worse for both sides than one surfaced honestly now.

11. How do you tailor your preparation and answers differently for a startup versus a large, established company?​

📖 Introduction​

The same underlying research habits from this chapter apply to both, but what interviewers at each tend to probe for shifts noticeably.

🎯 For a Startup: Comfort With Ambiguity and Broad Ownership​

Startup interviewers often probe for comfort with undefined processes, willingness to take on responsibilities outside a narrow job description, and genuine excitement about the specific problem the company is solving — the ambiguity-handling questions from the Problem Solving & Decision Making chapter are especially relevant to have ready here.

🎯 For a Large Company: Working Within Structure and Cross-Team Collaboration​

Larger, established company interviewers more often probe for effectiveness within existing processes, ability to collaborate across larger, more specialized teams, and a track record that demonstrates reliability at scale, rather than comfort with undefined scope.

💎 Good to Know: The Same Genuine Answer, Emphasized Differently, Usually Works for Both​

You don't necessarily need entirely different stories for each — the same real examples from your background, with the emphasis shifted toward ambiguity and ownership for a startup, or toward structure and cross-team collaboration for a larger company, generally cover both cases well.
