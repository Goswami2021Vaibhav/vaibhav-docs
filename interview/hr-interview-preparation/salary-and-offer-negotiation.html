Salary & Offer Negotiation

1. "What are your salary expectations?" How do you answer this when it's asked early in the process?​

📖 Introduction​

This question often comes up before an offer even exists, sometimes as early as a first HR screening call, which makes it feel premature — the goal is answering it usefully without boxing yourself in too early.

🎯 Give a Researched Range, Not a Single Fixed Number​

A range based on genuine market research for the role, level, and location — covered in the next question — anchors the conversation realistically without committing you to one exact figure this early, while a single number can either undersell you if too low or end the conversation prematurely if it reads as too high.

📝 Example Answer​

"Based on my research for this role and level in this market, I'm generally looking in the range of [researched range]. That said, I'm happy to discuss further once I understand the full scope of the role and the complete compensation package."

💎 Good to Know: Naming a Range You Haven't Actually Researched Is the Most Common Mistake Here​

A range pulled from a vague sense of "what sounds reasonable," rather than genuine market data, risks leaving real money on the table or naming something out of step with the actual market — the research covered next is what makes this answer credible rather than a guess.

2. How do you find out what a fair, market-appropriate salary range actually is before the conversation?​

📖 Introduction​

Every answer in this chapter depends on having a genuine, researched number in mind — this is the preparation the rest of the chapter assumes you've done, much like the company research covered in the previous chapter of this subject.

🧭 Where to Actually Look​

Salary aggregator sites and platforms that collect self-reported compensation data for the specific role, level, and location, cross-checked across more than one source since any single source can skew. Your own professional network, where a direct, discreet conversation with someone in a similar role can often surface more accurate, current numbers than public data. Recruiters themselves, who are often willing to share a rough range earlier in the process than candidates expect, if simply asked directly.

🎯 Account for Location, Level, and Company Size Specifically​

The same job title can have a genuinely wide range depending on these three factors — narrowing your research to numbers that actually match your specific situation, rather than a broad, generic figure for the title alone, produces a far more useful and defensible range.

💎 Good to Know: A Range Backed by Specific Sources Is Also More Persuasive in the Negotiation Itself​

Beyond helping you personally, being able to reference genuine market data during a later negotiation conversation — even without naming the exact source — makes your ask sound like an informed position rather than an arbitrary one.

3. Should you be the one to name a number first, or try to get the employer to share their range first?​

📖 Introduction​

This is a genuinely debated point in negotiation advice generally, and the honest answer is that both approaches are reasonable — what matters more is having a real number ready either way.

🎯 Try Redirecting the Question Back First, Politely​

A polite, direct redirect — "could you share the budgeted range for this role first?" — costs nothing to try and sometimes works, since the party who shares first can lose a little negotiating leverage.

🎯 If Pressed, Answer With Your Researched Range Rather Than Deflecting Indefinitely​

If the interviewer redirects back or presses for your number anyway, continuing to deflect starts to seem evasive — at that point, giving your genuine, researched range is the better move, since it keeps the conversation moving productively.

💎 Good to Know: This Matters Less Than Having a Real, Researched Number in the First Place​

Whichever party ends up naming a number first, the actual outcome of the negotiation depends far more on whether your number is genuinely well-researched than on the order in which it gets said out loud — this question is worth knowing the technique for, but not overthinking.

4. How do you handle being asked about your current or previous salary?​

📖 Introduction​

This question is asked less often than it used to be — many places have made it illegal for an employer to ask, specifically to prevent past pay from anchoring a new offer too low — but it still comes up, and it's worth having a ready answer either way.

🎯 You Can Decline to Share, Professionally​

"I'd prefer to focus on the market range for this specific role rather than my past compensation" is a professional, increasingly normal way to redirect the conversation, especially where the question isn't legally required to be answered.

🎯 If You Choose to Share, Frame It as a Data Point, Not a Ceiling​

If you do share a past figure, framing it explicitly as one data point rather than a cap — "my previous salary was [figure], though based on my research, the market range for this specific role is [higher range]" — helps prevent the past number from anchoring the new offer too low.

💎 Good to Know: Check Whether This Question Is Even Legally Permitted Where You're Interviewing​

Several regions and jurisdictions now specifically restrict employers from asking this question at all — worth being aware of your local rules, since in some cases you're not obligated to answer it regardless of how the question is framed.

5. Is it appropriate to negotiate an offer at all, and how do you do it professionally?​

📖 Introduction​

Some candidates worry that negotiating will seem greedy or risk losing the offer entirely — in reality, a reasonable, professionally framed negotiation is a completely normal, expected part of the process at most companies.

🎯 Express Genuine Enthusiasm First, Then Negotiate​

Opening with clear, genuine enthusiasm about the role and the offer overall, before raising the specific point you'd like to discuss, keeps the tone collaborative rather than adversarial — you're not rejecting the offer, you're refining it.

📝 Example Answer​

"I'm genuinely excited about this offer and about joining the team. Based on my research for this role and level, I was hoping we could discuss the base salary a bit further — is there flexibility to move it closer to [specific figure]?"

💎 Good to Know: Most Initial Offers Have Some Built-in Room for This Exact Conversation​

Many employers deliberately extend an initial offer with some room below their actual maximum, specifically expecting a negotiation conversation to follow — a reasonable counter is rarely a surprise to the other side, even if it can feel like a bold move to raise it.

6. How do you handle negotiating when you have a competing offer from another company?​

📖 Introduction​

A genuine competing offer is one of the strongest, most concrete pieces of leverage available in a negotiation, and it's worth using it directly rather than only hinting at it vaguely.

🎯 Mention It Directly, Without Necessarily Revealing Every Detail​

Stating plainly that you have another offer, and roughly what range or timeline is involved, is normal and effective — you don't need to disclose the other company's name or every specific detail unless you choose to.

📝 Example Answer​

"I want to be upfront that I have another offer in hand with a similar timeline, in a comparable range. This role is genuinely my preferred option, so I wanted to see if there's room to bring this offer closer to that range before I need to make a decision."

💎 Good to Know: Only Reference a Competing Offer That's Actually Real​

Referencing a fabricated competing offer to gain leverage is a genuinely risky move — it can be checked, directly or indirectly, and being caught in that specific lie tends to do far more damage than any negotiation gain would be worth.

7. What do you do if the initial offer comes in lower than you expected?​

📖 Introduction​

A lower-than-expected offer is disappointing but not necessarily final — how you respond in the moment matters more than the initial number itself.

🎯 Don't React or Decide on the Spot​

Taking a moment — even explicitly asking for a day or two to consider the full offer, covered later in this chapter — rather than reacting immediately or accepting out of surprise, gives you space to prepare a considered response rather than an emotional one.

🎯 Come Back With a Specific, Researched Counter, Not Just General Disappointment​

Rather than simply saying the offer feels low, returning with a specific number, backed by the same market research from earlier in this chapter, and a clear ask, gives the employer something concrete to actually respond to.

💎 Good to Know: A Lower Initial Offer Isn't Automatically a Sign the Company Won't Move​

Some organizations have a standard practice of starting below their actual budget, expecting a counter — a lower-than-hoped-for initial number is disappointing, but it's often still just the opening move of the conversation, not the company's final position.

8. How do you negotiate parts of an offer besides base salary — bonus, equity, joining bonus, remote work, vacation?​

📖 Introduction​

Base salary is often the least flexible part of an offer, since it's frequently tied to a formal pay band — other components can sometimes have more room to move, and are worth considering as part of the same conversation.

🎯 Know Which Levers Genuinely Exist Before Asking​

A signing bonus, additional equity, extra vacation days, a remote or hybrid work arrangement, or an accelerated review timeline are all real, commonly negotiable levers, some of which have more flexibility than base salary at a given company — worth asking generally which of these are on the table, if you're not sure.

🎯 Prioritize What Actually Matters Most to You, and Ask for That First​

If base salary genuinely has little room to move, shifting the conversation toward whichever of these other levers matters most to you personally, rather than pushing repeatedly on the one area that's actually fixed, makes for a more productive conversation overall.

💎 Good to Know: A One-Time Signing Bonus Can Sometimes Bridge a Gap When Base Salary Truly Can't Move​

When an employer says base salary is fixed by policy, a one-time signing bonus is often easier for them to approve, since it doesn't affect the ongoing pay band — worth proposing directly if the base number genuinely can't move.

9. What if the company says the offer is final and non-negotiable?​

📖 Introduction​

Sometimes this is genuinely true — a fixed pay band with no exceptions — and sometimes it's simply the opening position of the negotiation; either way, the right response is largely the same.

🎯 Ask One More Time, Specifically, Before Accepting That It's Final​

A single, direct, specific follow-up — naming the exact figure or component you're asking about one more time — costs little and occasionally still succeeds, even after an initial "the offer is final" response.

🎯 If It's Genuinely Final, Decide Based on the Offer as It Stands​

Once it's clear the offer truly won't move, the decision comes down to whether the offer as it stands is genuinely acceptable to you — continuing to push after a clear, final "no" tends to strain the relationship without changing the outcome.

💎 Good to Know: A Genuinely Final Offer Doesn't Mean the Negotiation Attempt Was a Mistake​

Asking reasonably and being told no costs very little in most cases — a professional, well-framed negotiation attempt that doesn't succeed rarely damages the relationship, as long as it was raised respectfully and accepted gracefully once the answer was clearly final.

10. How do you decline an offer gracefully if the numbers genuinely don't work?​

📖 Introduction​

Declining an offer is a completely normal outcome of a negotiation that didn't land where you needed it to, and handling it well matters, since the professional world is smaller than it seems.

🎯 Be Prompt, Grateful, and Clear​

Responding as soon as the decision is genuinely final, thanking them clearly for the offer and the time invested in the process, and stating plainly that the compensation doesn't work for you at this time, is a clean, professional way to close the conversation.

📝 Example Answer​

"Thank you again for the offer and for the time everyone spent throughout this process — I really enjoyed getting to know the team. After careful consideration, the compensation doesn't quite align with what I need to move forward right now, so I won't be able to accept. I'd genuinely love to stay in touch in case there's a fit down the line."

💎 Good to Know: Leaving the Door Open Is Usually Worth Doing, Genuinely​

Teams change, budgets change, and roles reopen — a graceful, warm decline, rather than a cold or resentful one, keeps a real possibility open for the future, and costs nothing to include if genuinely meant.

11. How much time is it reasonable to ask for before deciding on an offer, and how do you ask for it professionally?​

📖 Introduction​

Feeling pressured to decide on the spot is common, but rarely actually required — asking for reasonable time is a normal part of the process.

🎯 A Few Days to About a Week Is Generally Reasonable​

For most roles, asking for somewhere between a few days and about a week is a broadly accepted norm — enough time to genuinely consider the offer or compare it against another one, without stalling the company's process indefinitely.

🎯 Ask Directly, With a Specific Timeline​

"Thank you for the offer — could I have until [specific date] to review it fully and get back to you?" is a direct, professional way to ask, and naming a specific date, rather than an open-ended "some time," tends to be received better.

💎 Good to Know: Asking for Reasonable Time Rarely Costs You the Offer​

A genuine, well-reasoned request for a few days to review a significant decision is standard and expected — a company withdrawing an offer solely because a candidate asked for a reasonable amount of time to consider it is both rare and a meaningful signal about that company, if it does happen.
